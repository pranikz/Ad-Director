# Locale: US and UK (plus Canada, Ireland, Australia, New Zealand)

Read `international.md` first, then this file, before Phase 1 on a brief for these markets. It layers market specifics on top of `storytelling.md`, `realism.md` and `qa.md`.

## 1. What works here
Both markets reward **warm humour, specific people and craft**. Most recent US and UK Cannes film winners are funny, and the UK in particular rewards dry understatement. Purpose without a story, and celebrity without an idea, test poorly. Describe these archetypes; never copy a real ad or its lines:
| Archetype | Feel | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| **Big-game comedy (US)** | Super Bowl-scale spectacle: a simple premise, escalating gags and a twist ending, teased online before the game. Director can't use real celebrities, so build an original, larger-than-life character instead | Snacks, cars, apps, insurance, delivery, telecom buying fame |
| **Recurring spokescharacter (US)** | One character and a running gag that returns for years | Insurance, telecom, QSR, household brands |
| **Christmas epic (UK)** | A 60–120 s emotional story, released early to mid November, with a reveal and a slow cover song; the country rates them like films | Department stores, grocers, retail, parcel and telecom brands |
| **Dry wit (UK)** | Deadpan, self-deprecating, everyday awkwardness played small; the joke is what isn't said | Banks, telcos, supermarkets, utilities, almost anything |
| **Main Street hero (US)** | A diner owner, contractor or family business with grit and optimism, and the product as their quiet ally | Banking, payments, B2B, trucks, tools |
| **Kitchen-table chaos** | A real family talking over each other at breakfast; the product fixes one small mess | FMCG, grocery, insurance, telecom, streaming |
| **Sports-day ritual** | Tailgates, Friday-night football, the pub screen or the five-a-side, focused on fans rather than stars | Snacks, telecom, cars, soft drinks |

## 2. Moments and calendar
- **US:** the Super Bowl (early February, with ads released online days before), Valentine's Day, March Madness, Easter, Mother's Day (May), Memorial Day, graduation, Father's Day, the 4th of July, back to school (July–August), Labor Day, Halloween, Thanksgiving with Black Friday and Cyber Monday, and "the holidays" (say "holidays" unless the brief says Christmas, Hanukkah or Kwanzaa).
- **US heritage months:** Black History Month (February), Women's History Month (March), Pride (June), Hispanic Heritage Month (mid September to mid October), Native American Heritage Month (November). Use them only when the brand has a real, ongoing commitment. One-off campaigns draw backlash from both sides.
- **US sport:** the NFL season (September–February), NBA, MLB, college football, and the 2028 Los Angeles Olympics.
- **UK:** the Christmas ad season (November), Bonfire Night (5 November), Boxing Day sales, Burns Night, Pancake Day, **Mothering Sunday in March** (not May), Easter, bank holidays, summer festivals, Wimbledon, the Six Nations, the Premier League (August–May) and Black Friday.
- **Canada:** Canada Day (1 July), Thanksgiving in October, hockey season.
- **Australia and New Zealand:** the seasons are flipped, so Christmas is summer (beach, barbecue). Australia Day (26 January) is contested; avoid celebratory framing. Also the AFL and NRL grand finals and the Melbourne Cup.
- **Elections:** political ads have their own rules, and many US states require AI disclosure in them. Stay out of politics unless the brief is political.

## 3. Language and dialogue
| Market | Write dialogue in | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| US | American English | US spelling on supers (color, center, Mom). Write dates with the month spelled out |
| US Hispanic | Spanish, or Spanglish as people actually speak it | Pick the community (Mexican-American, Puerto Rican, Cuban, Dominican…) and cast to match. Disclosures go in Spanish when the ad is in Spanish |
| UK | British English | UK spelling (colour, centre, Mum). Write the accent into the cast line: Scottish, Welsh, Geordie, Scouse, Brummie, Mancunian, Multicultural London English. Avoid the "posh means clever, northern means comic" shortcut |
| Wales | English, or Welsh on request | — |
| Ireland | Irish English | Irish idiom, not British; Irish-language words only if the brief wants them |
| Canada | English, plus **Quebec French** for Quebec | Quebec law requires French in advertising there. Use Quebec French, not France French |
| Australia, NZ | Australian or NZ English | Slang sparingly; te reo Māori words in NZ only when the brief asks |

## 4. Casting
- **Cast the real mix of the place:** a Houston taqueria, a Queens bodega, a Leeds terrace or a Birmingham high street. Vary race, age, body, ability and class.
- **US:** avoid typecasting (Black characters only in sport or music, Latino characters only in service jobs, Asian characters only in tech). Multiracial families are ordinary; don't make them a statement.
- **UK:** the ASA's harmful gender stereotypes rule applies. No useless dads, no women solely responsible for the cleaning, no children's activities or careers framed as for one gender. Humour is no defence.
- **Models drift to American suburbia for "UK" briefs.** Write UK cues: terraced houses, kettles, radiators, switched plug sockets, school uniforms, and **right-hand-drive cars driving on the left** (also in Ireland, Australia and New Zealand).
- **Write class into wardrobe** (a hi-vis vest, a supermarket fleece, a waxed country jacket, a work polo with a name badge), and keep it respectful.

## 5. Places (name the city and the spot)
- **US:** a Queens bodega, a Brooklyn brownstone stoop, a Texas Hill Country barbecue joint, a Midwest diner, an Ohio cul-de-sac, an LA taco-truck lot, a Little Havana café in Miami, a New Orleans shotgun-house porch, a Phoenix strip mall, a Minnesota ice-fishing lake, a college-town laundromat.
- **UK:** a Glasgow tenement close, a Manchester terraced street, a London corner shop or chicken shop, a Leeds working men's club, a Cornish harbour, a Welsh valleys rugby club, a Birmingham balti house, a Blackpool chip shop, a commuter train carriage, a Belfast café.
- **Avoid the postcard:** no Manhattan skyline, Golden Gate, red phone box or Big Ben unless the brief wants the icon.
- **Get the hardware right:** US outlets, yellow school buses and mailboxes; UK plugs, the steering wheel on the right and traffic on the left. Plates stay people-free, with no readable text or brand names.

## 6. Cultural sensitivity (fail QA on any of these)
- **US:** race and policing, guns (none unless the brief says so), religion, partisan politics, Native American imagery as a mascot or costume, other cultures' festivals as costume, 9/11 imagery, military service played for laughs, and trauma used to sell. Identity-led campaigns can trigger organised boycotts from either side; only run them when the brand's commitment is real and long-term.
- **UK:** class mockery that punches down, depictions of real royals or anything implying royal endorsement, the Remembrance poppy used commercially, sectarian football colours in Scotland, Troubles imagery in Northern Ireland, and anything that spoils Santa for children.
- **Australia:** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander imagery only with Indigenous creative involvement.

## 7. Claims, supers and compliance (check against the brief and the current rules)
**US: the FTC, self-regulation and network clearance**
- **Truth and substantiation:** no deceptive or unfair claims (FTC Act Section 5). Hold the evidence before you claim; health claims need scientific evidence.
- **Clear and conspicuous disclosures:** in the same medium as the claim (a spoken claim gets a spoken disclosure, an on-screen claim an on-screen one), big enough, on screen long enough, near the claim, unavoidable, and in the ad's language. A tiny corner super or a caption link doesn't count.
- **Endorsements (2023 Guides):** disclose any material connection; endorsements must reflect real experience; show typical results.
- **Fake reviews and testimonials (2024 rule):** banned, AI-generated ones included.
- **"Made in USA"** means all or virtually all. "Free", prices and auto-renewals need clear terms.
- **Children:** COPPA and the CARU guidelines (BBB National Programs).
- **Self-regulation and clearance:** competitors challenge claims at NAD (BBB National Programs), and TV networks' clearance departments review the script and substantiation before air.
- **Sectors:** alcohol (TTB rules and industry codes, which set minimum ages for anyone shown drinking and ban any link to excess or driving); prescription drugs (FDA: fair balance and a major statement of risks); financial products (SEC, FINRA, CFPB); political ads ("paid for by" and state AI-disclosure laws).
- **AI and likeness:** New York's synthetic-performer disclosure law; state right-of-publicity and digital-replica laws (New York, California, and Tennessee's ELVIS Act on voices). No lookalikes or soundalikes.

**UK: ASA/CAP and Clearcast**
- **The codes:** the CAP Code (non-broadcast, including online and social) and the BCAP Code (TV and radio). Ads must be legal, decent, honest and truthful, and you must hold documentary evidence before publishing.
- **Clearcast:** TV ads need Clearcast clearance before they air. Submit the script early with the substantiation. Clearcast also checks that supers are legible and held long enough.
- **Qualifications** must clarify the claim, never contradict it.
- **Harmful gender stereotypes** are banned (since June 2019).
- **Less-healthy food and drink:** since 5 January 2026, ads for identifiable less-healthy products can't run on TV between 5:30 am and 9 pm, or as paid online ads at any time. Brand ads that show no identifiable less-healthy product are exempt, so make a brand-only edit when the brief needs one.
- **Alcohol:** anyone drinking or playing a significant role must be, and look, over 25. No link to social or sexual success, daring or driving.
- **Gambling:** no strong appeal to under-18s, and no under-25s gambling or playing a significant role.
- **Environmental claims:** the ASA and the CMA's Green Claims Code require full life-cycle evidence for absolute claims ("sustainable", "carbon neutral"). The CMA can now fine directly for misleading consumers.
- **AI:** the ASA's 2026 guidance applies the existing rules in full. Filters and AI must not exaggerate results, and AI depictions of real people must not imply endorsement.

**Canada, Australia, New Zealand:** self-regulatory codes (Ad Standards in Canada and Australia, the ASA in New Zealand) plus consumer law (the Competition Bureau in Canada, the ACCC in Australia); Quebec's French-language rules; health products under Health Canada or the TGA.

## 8. Formats and deliverables
- **US TV and CTV:** 30 and 15 s dominate, with 60 s for tentpoles and 6 s on streaming. The Super Bowl gets an online teaser, an extended cut and the on-air 30 or 60.
- **UK TV:** 30, 20 and 10 s, each with a Clearcast clock number. The Christmas film runs 60–120 s online and in the first break, then cuts down.
- **Social:** as in `international.md` section 7.
- **End card:** one CTA, the URL or app badge, the legal lines from the brief, and the AI-disclosure super for New York and anywhere else it's needed. Use US or UK spelling to match the market.
