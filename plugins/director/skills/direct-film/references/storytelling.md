# Storytelling: stories that carry a message without feeling like ads

For Indian briefs (the default), read `locales/india.md` first. It lists the archetypes that work in India (wordplay, mohalla business, family, aspiration, jugaad, festive, absurd, cricket) and the calendar.

For any other market, read `locales/international.md` (the `director-international` agent's default) and the region file it points to. They list the archetypes that travel (absurd hero, domestic twist, problem-solved demo, mockumentary, holiday short film and more) and each region's own (the UK Christmas ad, the Ramadan family film, the Thai tearjerker, the festive homecoming).

## The method (any brief)
1. **Find the device.** It is the one idea that the product or message, its benefit and everyday life share. Common kinds:
   - **Word or pun:** a word that means one thing in daily speech and another in the product. A bank's "*Lite*" current account: light chai, bad light, "current gaya".
   - **Human truth:** something everyone does or feels. "Nobody reads the manual." "Moms pack one extra roti."
   - **Tension:** the problem the product removes, shown at its most human moment. A supplier wants cash now; the phone dies at the station.
   - **Ritual or culture:** a festival, a commute, match day, a wedding. The product rides a moment people already love.
   - **Exaggeration:** the benefit pushed to a ridiculous but believable extreme.
   If a reference film exists, **keep its device and change everything else.**
2. **Brainstorm wide.** List 15–20 everyday situations where the device naturally comes up, then keep the ones that contain a conflict or a turn.
3. **Cast the real user.** Put each situation with a person who would actually use the product: a shopkeeper, a nurse, a student, a farmer, a grandmother, a delivery rider. Real customers are not models.
4. **The message is the twist, not the explanation.** The situation plays the device in its everyday sense, and the payoff flips it to the product sense in one line or one image.
5. **Map each benefit to one beat.** If the brief has three benefits, spread them across the set rather than cramming all three into one film.

## Structures by length
| Length | Shots | Beats |
|---|---|---|
| 6–10 s (bumper) | 2–3 | Setup → twist with product |
| 15 s | 4 | Setup with the device → pressure → product beat → payoff and held frame |
| 20–30 s | 5–7 | Setup → complication → escalation → product beat → payoff → topper |
| 45–60 s | 8–12 | Mini-story with a clear want, an obstacle, the product's role, change, and resolution |

Add 4–10 s of end card for exact claims and the CTA: an optional offer beat, then the packshot.

## Tone playbooks
- **Comedy:** deadpan hero and over-reacting side character, set-as-joke, one tiny topper after the payoff. Dry timing beats slapstick.
- **Warm:** a small specific gesture instead of speeches, silence before the payoff, one prop that carries meaning. No melodrama.
- **Documentary:** real-feeling routines and imperfect moments, with the product used naturally and not shown off.
- **Cinematic:** one striking image per beat, restraint in dialogue, the product revealed late.

## Rules
- **Specific beats generic.** Write "Bhagirath Palace at dusk", not "a market"; "64, big-boned, flour on her hands", not "a woman".
- **Diversity by default.** Across a set, mix ages from teens to 70s, men and women as heroes, regions, communities, body types and abilities, without stereotypes.
- **One mechanic per film.** Never reuse a joke or emotion mechanic within a set.
- **Kids** appear playing, never working, always fully dressed, never in peril.
- **No mocking the customer.** The hero is clever, calm or kind, and the product is their ally.
- **Claims:** dialogue gives the feeling ("kama bhi raha hai" — it's earning too), and the end card gives the facts.
